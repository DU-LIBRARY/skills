# Our Identity as Young Muslims

## 1. Opening

Who are we, and what kind of Muslims do we want to become?

## 2. More than grades

As students of the Imam Muhammad Adam Institute, we are here for more than exams, good grades or a good career. We are here to strengthen our faith, gain knowledge, build good character and prepare for the responsibilities ahead of us.

Islam has given us a remarkable history, a rich intellectual heritage and, above all, a way of life with purpose and direction. From Spain and North Africa, through the Middle East, to Indonesia and China, Muslim men and women of many backgrounds built on the discoveries of earlier civilisations, invented new things and pushed human knowledge further. Our aim is to become principled Muslims whose knowledge, character and actions benefit the people around us.

Six qualities describe who we are and who we want to be.

## 3. Strong in Faith: our Islamic identity

Faith sits at the centre of our lives. Being Muslim means more than having a Muslim name, dressing a certain way or coming from a Muslim family. It means knowing Allah, loving His Messenger ﷺ and trying to live by His guidance. Our faith answers life's biggest questions: where we came from, why we are here and where we are going.

As young Muslims, we try to:

- **Anchor ourselves in worship.** Build a strong bond with Allah through ṣalāh, the Qurʾān and sincere remembrance, starting with wuḍūʾ. Muslim engineers in the 13th century even built automated water dispensers for it.
- **Understand our beliefs.** Learn what we believe in depth, and why.
- **Live the Sunnah.** Love the Prophet ﷺ and follow his example, in big decisions and in small daily habits such as using the miswāk before prayer.
- **Stand firm.** Stay confident in who we are when we are tested, remembering that Allah sees what we do in public and in private.

We never feel apologetic or embarrassed about being Muslim. Our faith is something to treasure, understand and live by.

## 4. Seekers of Knowledge: our scholarly identity

Islam encourages curiosity, reflection and the courage to think. The first word revealed to the Prophet ﷺ was a command to learn:

اقْرَأْ بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ
"Read in the name of your Lord Who created." (Sūrat al-ʿAlaq: 1)

Muslims who took this command seriously asked bold questions, tested ideas and changed the world. In 9th-century Baghdad, Caliph al-Maʾmūn brought scholars of every faith to the House of Wisdom to translate Greek, Persian and Syriac works. In Fez, Fāṭimah al-Fihrī founded al-Qarawiyyīn, the world's oldest university still operating. Ibn Sīnā wrote medical encyclopaedias that were studied for centuries. Al-Zahrāwī designed more than 200 surgical instruments, many still recognisable in operating theatres today. Jābir ibn Ḥayyān laid the foundations of experimental chemistry.

In astronomy, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī recorded the Andromeda galaxy and mapped the stars, and Maryam al-Aṣṭurlābiyya and Ulugh Beg made astrolabes and star tables of great precision. In mathematics and light, al-Khwārizmī gave the world algebra, al-Kindī pioneered codebreaking, al-Bīrūnī worked on mathematical problems, and Ibn al-Haytham transformed optics with his camera obscura experiments. In geography and engineering, al-Idrīsī mapped the world, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa travelled more than 75,000 miles across 44 of today's countries, ʿAbbās ibn Firnās attempted winged flight, and al-Jazarī and Taqī al-Dīn built mechanical clocks, crankshafts and water pumps centuries ahead of their time.

They did all this with parchment, ink and hard work. We have every book, laboratory and digital tool at our fingertips. So why shouldn't the next great doctor, engineer, mathematician, researcher or scholar be sitting in this room?

As students, we try to:

- **Love all useful learning.** Bring the same energy to the Islamic sciences and to our school subjects.
- **Question and reflect.** Think critically, ask deep questions and aim to understand, not just memorise.
- **Honour our heritage.** Respect our teachers, and treat knowledge as an amānah, a trust to use responsibly.

Knowledge without character leads to arrogance. Knowledge guided by faith and humility benefits everyone.

## 5. Noble in Character: our identity of adab and akhlāq

The real test of our education is our character. The Prophet ﷺ was humanity's greatest teacher and its finest example of good character:

وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍ
"And indeed, you are upon an outstanding standard of character." (Sūrat al-Qalam: 4)

Good character is how we behave when nobody is watching, how we treat people weaker than us and how we respond when our patience is tested. Islamic teaching even shaped everyday care and hygiene: ḥammāms (public baths), soap made from oils and al-qalī, al-Kindī's 107 methods for making perfume, Ziryāb's early toothpaste in al-Andalus, and al-Zahrāwī's writing on oral care, skin and grooming.

As students, we try to:

- **Respect everyone.** Parents, teachers, elders and classmates alike.
- **Live with integrity.** Be truthful, trustworthy and sincere in what we say and promise.
- **Choose humility.** Reject pride, arrogance and looking down on others.
- **Control ourselves.** Manage our anger, be patient, own our mistakes and treat every person with dignity.

Good grades may win applause, but good character earns lasting respect. A student with top marks who lacks honesty, humility and manners has missed the point of education.

## 6. Confident in Society: our identity as British Muslims

As Muslims in Britain, we are an active part of the society around us. Being a committed Muslim and contributing to British life support each other.

When the Prophet ﷺ established the community of Madīnah, Muslims lived alongside neighbours of other faiths under clear agreements on rights, justice and shared responsibility.

Muslim merchants, navigators and thinkers connected Africa, Asia and Europe. They exchanged ideas, improved navigation, spread crops such as citrus fruits, apricots and rice, shared water systems like qanats and irrigation rules that left their mark on Spain, and carried Yemeni coffee around the world, all the way to London's coffeehouses, the "penny universities".

As young British Muslims, we try to:

- **Walk with quiet confidence.** Be proud of being Muslim, without cutting ourselves off and without compromising.
- **Build bridges.** Respect people of all faiths and backgrounds and make real friendships, while staying true to our principles.
- **Stand up for justice.** Take responsibility for our local community and speak up for fairness, even when it is unpopular.
- **Lead by example.** Let our courtesy, honesty and warmth show people what Islam looks like.

We don't watch from the sidelines. Rooted in our faith, we step forward as trustworthy neighbours, thoughtful citizens and people who work for the common good.

## 7. A Life of Service: our identity of khidmah and responsibility

Islam measures greatness by what we give to others, not by what we collect for ourselves:

وَأَحْسِنْ كَمَا أَحْسَنَ اللَّهُ إِلَيْكَ
"And do good as Allah has done good to you." (Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ: 77)

In classical Muslim societies, faith led to institutions for people's welfare: public hospitals (bīmāristāns) with specialist wards, teaching hospitals and early mental health care, such as the hospital of Aḥmad ibn Ṭūlūn in Egypt, along with regulated pharmacies, water-raising systems that brought clean water to farms and towns, and public gardens.

Each of us has our own talents and opportunities. Whether we become ʿulamāʾ, teachers, doctors, engineers, business owners or leaders in other fields, our abilities are blessings meant to help others.

As students, we try to:

- **Care deeply.** Feel real concern for people who are struggling, disadvantaged or overlooked.
- **Serve first.** Take responsibility for our families, neighbourhoods and wider society.
- **Solve real problems.** Use our skills to challenge injustice and make practical changes.
- **Rethink leadership.** True leadership is khidmah, service, never ego or status.

Success isn't counted in money, titles or trophies. It is measured by the lives made better because we were here.

## 8. Striving for Excellence: our identity of iḥsān

Islam leaves no room for laziness, carelessness or half-hearted effort. It asks us for iḥsān: excellence, sincerity and skill in everything we do.

إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَتَبَ الْإِحْسَانَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ
"Indeed, Allah has prescribed excellence in all things." (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Look at what Muslim craftsmen and thinkers produced: the geometry of the Alhambra in Spain, Mimar Sinan's domes at the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne and the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul, fine ceramics, lustreware and carpets, the philosophy of Ibn Rushd, the history and sociology of Ibn Khaldūn, and the poetry of Rūmī and Saʿdī. None of it came from doing the minimum. It came from discipline, patience and the wish to do things properly.

Whether we are memorising the Qurʾān, solving equations, learning Arabic, playing sport or helping at home, we bring the same spirit of iḥsān.

As students, we try to:

- **Give our best.** Approach our studies with focus, punctuality and self-discipline.
- **Refuse to coast.** Never settle for "good enough" when we can do better.
- **Learn from setbacks.** Treat mistakes as steps forward, not reasons to give up.
- **Stay ambitious and grateful.** Aim high while staying humble and thankful to Allah.

Our predecessors achieved great things because they valued knowledge, worked hard and made sacrifices. We are here to write our own chapter, not only to admire theirs.

## 9. Our heritage, our responsibility

We belong to an ummah that changed the world, and it did so because our predecessors understood that knowledge, power and progress must be guided by faith, justice and moral responsibility. Without those anchors, knowledge becomes a tool for exploitation and power becomes a means of oppression.

At the Imam Muhammad Adam Institute, we inherit a living scholarly tradition while preparing for the modern world. Our job is to carry that tradition forward: proud of our history without living in the past, confident in our beliefs without becoming arrogant, and ambitious in our goals without forgetting our duty to Allah and His creation.

## 10. The six qualities to remember

- **Strong in Faith:** know Allah, love His Messenger ﷺ and live by Islam with conviction.
- **Seekers of Knowledge:** love learning, think deeply and treat knowledge as a trust.
- **Noble in Character:** respect, honesty, humility and compassion, even when no one is watching.
- **Confident in Society:** stand tall as British Muslims and contribute to the common good.
- **Committed to Service:** turn your talents into khidmah that helps families and communities.
- **Striving for Excellence:** pursue iḥsān, with discipline, skill and sincerity, in everything you do.

## 11. A final thought

Years from now, people may not remember our exam grades or the prizes we won. They will remember how we treated them: whether we kept our word, whether we stood up for what was right, and whether we made their lives better.

Let us be a generation firm in faith, rich in knowledge, noble in character, confident in our identity and devoted to serving others. That is the standard we work towards every day at the Imam Muhammad Adam Institute.
