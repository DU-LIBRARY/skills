Ibn Baṭṭūṭa

Muslims who took this command seriously asked bold questions, tested ideas and changed the world. In 9th-century Baghdad, Caliph al-Maʾmūn brought scholars of every faith to the House of Wisdom to translate Greek, Persian and Syriac works. In Fez, Fāṭimah al-Fihrī founded al-Qarawiyyīn, the world's oldest university still operating. Ibn Sīnā wrote medical encyclopaedias that were studied for centuries. Al-Zahrāwī designed more than 200 surgical instruments, many still recognisable in operating theatres today. Jābir ibn Ḥayyān laid the foundations of experimental chemistry. In astronomy, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī recorded the Andromeda galaxy and mapped the stars, and Maryam al-Aṣṭurlābiyya and Ulugh Beg made astrolabes and star tables of great precision. In mathematics and light, al-Khwārizmī gave the world algebra, al-Kindī pioneered codebreaking, al-Bīrūnī worked on mathematical problems, and Ibn al-Haytham transformed optics with his camera obscura experiments. In geography and engineering, al-Idrīsī mapped the world, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa travelled more than 75,000 miles across 44 of today's countries, ʿAbbās ibn Firnās attempted winged flight, and al-Jazarī and Taqī al-Dīn built mechanical clocks, crankshafts and water pumps centuries ahead of their time.
