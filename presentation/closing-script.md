# Closing segment: Madanī's principles, then and now

## Opening transition

As-salāmu ʿalaykum wa-raḥmatullāhi wa-barakātuh. You've just heard the proofs from the Arabic language, the Qur'an and the Sunnah. My part is about practice. First I'll go through the principles Mawlānā Ḥusayn Aḥmad Madanī set out in his letter to Ḥāfiẓ Muḥammad Ṣiddīq, and how he applied them to India. Then I'll look at how Muslims in Britain can use the same framework today.

## The principles

From the letter we can draw out nine principles:

1. A uniting factor
2. Equal citizenship in a joint state
3. Choosing the lesser evil
4. Constitutional and legal protections
5. Local and regional autonomy
6. Educational freedom and civic engagement
7. Continuous moral and civic effort
8. Principled foreign and public policy
9. Conditional cooperation

## Part 1: How Madanī applied them to India

In the letter, Mawlānā Madanī applies each of these to India.

**First, the uniting factor.** He found one thing every Indian, Muslim or not, could unite around: ending colonial rule. He was a pragmatist. Muslims on their own didn't have the strength to remove the colonial power, so he worked to bring all Indians into the same struggle.

**Second, equal citizenship in a joint state.** Whether independent India ended up united or federal, he said, it would be a joint government in which Muslims and non-Muslims had equal rights and equal duties. He was also clear that this would not be an Islamic system of government.

**Third, the lesser evil.** For him, a united, independent India was better than colonial rule, because Muslims would have more room to uphold the truth and practise their religion. Islamic law couldn't be implemented under a joint government, but that government would still give Muslims the chance to show the superiority of Islamic law, in argument and in conduct, and to win non-Muslims over to it. He also preferred a united India to partition. Dividing the country, he warned, would let the colonial power exploit the rivalry and keep its grip on both, and the fear of non-Muslims in a united India was unfounded. And the joint government was never the end goal.

**Fourth, constitutional and legal protections.** He pictured a democratic constitution with an elected president, who could be Muslim or not, and a government that guaranteed political, economic and religious rights, personal law in the provinces where Muslims were a minority, and legal protection for mosques, awqāf, graveyards and Muslim culture.

**Fifth, local and regional autonomy.** He wanted the centre limited to defence, foreign affairs, transport and some economic powers. Social and cultural matters would go to the provinces, and the five Muslim-majority provinces would be free to make their own laws on education, the economy and social and cultural life.

**Sixth, educational freedom and civic engagement.** Education was a provincial matter, so Muslims in minority provinces could ask for special provision for religious education, even if it meant paying extra tax. Shared schools and colleges were fine as long as the syllabus didn't clash with Islamic values.

**Seventh, continuous moral and civic effort.** A joint government wasn't going to implement the Sharīʿah, and the lesser evil wasn't the final goal. So Muslims still needed Iṣlāḥ (reform) centres and steady intellectual and moral work, with their eyes on the real goal.

**Eighth, principled foreign and public policy.** Madanī argued that real Muslim participation at the centre, Muslim government in the provinces and ties with neighbouring Muslim countries would keep India's foreign policy friendly to Islam, and leave Indian Muslims better placed to help free other Muslim lands.

**Ninth and last, conditional cooperation.** Working with non-Muslims for independence was permissible, and necessary, as long as certain things were safeguarded: Muslim participation in government, personal law, religious and cultural institutions, and self-rule in the five Muslim-majority provinces.

## Part 2: Applying Madanī's framework in Britain today

So how do we bring Mawlānā Madanī's ideas into the lives of British Muslims today?

**First, the uniting factor.** In India, ending colonial rule was the one thing that united everyone. We don't live under colonial occupation, so what unites us here is the everyday pressure ordinary people in Britain are under. What are those pressures? Recent UK polling puts the cost of living at the top, at 90%, rising to 94% among 16-to-29-year-olds and 92% among women. The NHS comes next at 79%, and 83% among the over-70s. Then the wider economy at 71%. Younger adults are also worried about housing (65%) and jobs (63%), and older adults about crime (69%) and global instability (64%). So rather than working only with Muslim groups, British Muslims can work with trade unions, tenants' associations, patient groups, local charities and interfaith networks: running food banks and warm hubs together, campaigning for NHS funding and affordable housing, and organising neighbourhood safety schemes with local residents.

**Second, equal citizenship.** British Muslims are equal citizens in a democracy, with the same rights, the same duties and the same voice as everyone else. Freedom of religion is already part of UK law. Compare France, where a strict model of laïcité restricts visible religious dress such as the hijab in state schools and public-sector jobs. Britain generally makes room for religious dress, prayer at work and a public faith identity.

**Third, the lesser evil.** We can't expect an Islamic state in Britain. But we can't afford to step back from politics either, and leave decisions about our schools, the economy and our basic freedoms to other people. Taking part in elections and public life, even when no party is perfect, protects our communities far better than withdrawing.

**Fourth, legal protections.** We rely on, and defend, the safeguards UK law already gives us: equality and anti-discrimination law, the rules on charities and waqfs, and protection for mosques, Muslim cemeteries and the family practices British law allows.

**Fifth, local autonomy.** We have nothing like Madanī's five Muslim-majority provinces. Our autonomy is local: councils, school governing bodies, mosque committees, neighbourhood forums and voluntary groups, where communities shape local services and cultural life.

**Sixth, education.** Parents and teachers can set up faith schools and supplementary maktabs within the regulations, and play an active part in state schools, colleges and universities.

**Seventh, moral and civic effort.** Political engagement never replaces Iṣlāḥ, inner reform. That means investing in mentoring young people, in strong families and in honest conduct in everyday life.

**Eighth, foreign and public policy.** By voting, writing to MPs and taking part in public debate, British Muslims can argue for fair policies at home, and for a humane foreign policy and humanitarian aid abroad.

**Ninth and last, conditional cooperation.** Madanī's lesson for us is to work wholeheartedly with the rest of society on the common good, such as the NHS, the cost of living and local services, while holding firm to our religious principles, our institutions and our moral limits.

## Closing

To close the whole presentation: from the classical proofs you heard earlier, to Madanī's plan for a united India, to our lives in Britain today, composite nationalism gives us a practical way forward. We can be faithful Muslims and active, responsible citizens at the same time.
