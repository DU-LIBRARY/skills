# Mawlānā Madanī’s ten applications, and Britain today

All ten points come from a letter Mawlānā Madanī raḥimahullāh wrote to Ḥāfiẓ Muḥammad Ṣiddīq, which is printed near the end of Naqsh-e-Hayat. I’ve checked each one against the letter itself, and where our summary didn’t quite match what he wrote, I’ve mentioned it.

## How he applied it to India

### 1. Standing against oppression, but peacefully

So the first thing Mawlānā says is that British rule over India was oppressive, and Muslims had a duty to work towards freeing the country. But he's very realistic about it. He says, look, Muslims simply don't have the strength to remove this government by force. So the duty on every Muslim right now is to struggle peacefully. The goal is freedom, and the way to get there is peaceful agitation.

(Note for us: in the letter he first mentions that, in principle, resistance can range from boycotts to armed struggle, and only then narrows it to peaceful means. He also calls India “Darul Harb”, but only as a technical fiqh term, and only for as long as British rule lasts. In front of the MP, either explain it that way or leave it out.)

### 2. Strategic unity

Then he asks the obvious question: what happens if Muslims take on the British on their own? They lose, and they suffer badly for it. So they have to join hands with the other communities of India. And there's a nice point here. Because everyone struggles together, everyone shares in the freedom at the end. A shared problem needs a shared effort.

### 3. Equal citizenship in a joint state

Next, he's very honest about what this new India would look like. Whether it ends up unitary or federal, it's going to be a joint government, where Muslims and non-Muslims have equal rights and equal duties. And he doesn't dress it up. He says plainly that a system like this “cannot be called the Islamic system of government.” I think that honesty is one of the most striking things about the letter.

### 4. Choosing the lesser evil

So why accept it? Because it's the lesser evil. A free, united India gives Muslims far more room to practise and share their faith than British rule ever did. And he goes further. He says it's a lesser evil than Partition too, because dividing the country would just let Britain play India and Pakistan off against each other. And as for the fear of living alongside a non-Muslim majority, he says that fear is unfounded. When the ideal isn't on the table, you choose the option that does the least harm and leaves the most room for good.

### 5. Constitutional and legal protections

But he's not naive either. He wants it all written down. He expects a democratic constitution, with an elected President serving a fixed term, sometimes a Muslim and sometimes not, and without the absolute powers of a king. Muslims would have guaranteed political, economic and religious rights. Where they're a minority, they keep their personal law. And their mosques, awqāf, graveyards and culture are protected by law. So cooperation, yes, but backed by guarantees.

(Note for us: our original summary said the presidency would rotate, but the letter only says it would sometimes be a Muslim and sometimes not.)

### 6. Local and regional autonomy

Then he talks about where power should sit. The central government should only handle the big things, like defence, foreign affairs, transport and some economic matters. Everything social and cultural goes to the provinces, and the five Muslim-majority provinces would be free to make their own laws on education, the economy and social life. The idea is simple: keep the centre limited, so people can shape the things closest to their own lives.

### 7. Educational freedom and engagement

On education, he says it should be a provincial matter. Where Muslims are a minority, they can arrange their own religious education, even if that means paying extra or running their own institutions. But at the same time, he has no problem with Muslims studying alongside everyone else, as long as the syllabus doesn't clash with Islamic values. Because, as he puts it, staying away from cooperation with others in the country is “quite impossible.”

### 8. The work carries on

Now, this next point is important. He's clear that a joint government won't bring about Islamic law, and he says “the lesser evil cannot be the ultimate goal for Muslims.” In other words, independence isn't the finish line. The work of daʿwah, of reform, of setting a good example, all of that carries on. In his words, “The road to action is open for Muslims.” He even talks about setting up Iṣlāḥ, or reform, centres to look after the community's values.

### 9. A principled foreign policy

He also looks beyond India's borders. He says independence is more than just a national issue. If Muslims have a real say in central government, run several provinces and have good ties with neighbouring Muslim countries, then India's foreign policy will be friendly to the Muslim world. And free Indian Muslims will be in a much better position to help Muslims elsewhere.

### 10. Conditional cooperation

And finally, he sums it all up. Working with non-Muslims for independence is, in his words, “permissible and necessary.” But it comes with conditions: real Muslim participation in government, protection of personal law, legal protection for mosques, awqāf and graveyards, and Muslim-majority government in five of the eleven provinces. So it's not a blank cheque. The cooperation rests on safeguards for faith and community.

## What this looks like in Britain today

### 1. Something that unites us

In Mawlānā’s India, what brought people together was a shared burden, colonial rule. In Britain there’s no oppressor to unite against. What unites us is the everyday problems that affect Muslims and their neighbours equally. Leaving immigration aside, the latest ONS figures (August 2026) show what people care about most: the cost of living (89%), the NHS (79%), the economy (70%), climate change (62%), crime (58%) and housing (54%). For young people, housing and jobs come up just as much. No community can fix any of these on its own, and every community suffers when they’re left.

### 2. Working with other communities

Here are some practical ideas, along with the problems we’d need to think about:

- Cost of living: food banks, warm spaces and debt or benefits advice run jointly by mosques, churches, gurdwaras, temples and synagogues. The catch is that we shouldn’t duplicate what already exists, so it’s better to partner with local providers. It also has to last beyond the winter. And help for non-Muslims comes from general charity (ṣadaqah), not zakāh.
- The NHS and health: health days at the mosque with local NHS teams, and encouraging young people into NHS careers. Some health topics, like organ donation, involve differences among scholars, so our role is to inform and refer to the ʿulamāʾ, not to campaign. Women’s health work needs to be led by women.
- Crime and youth safety: mentoring, sport and safe spaces for young people across communities, and good relationships with the police and council. Trust in the police isn’t the same in every community, so it has to be built steadily. Everything needs proper safeguarding, and we should measure whether it’s actually working.
- Housing and jobs: housing advice sessions, CV clinics, and apprenticeships with local employers.
- Trust in politics: joint hustings and voter registration drives. But mosques and faith charities have to stay strictly non-party-political, as charity law doesn’t allow them to back a party or candidate.

A few principles tie all of this together. First, we cooperate in civic life, not in worship. That’s Mawlānā’s own line between qawm and millat. Second, it has to go beyond a handful of community “leaders” and involve women, young people and families. Third, relationships need to be built before a crisis, not during one. Leicester in 2022 showed that. And finally, one-off interfaith events fade, but ongoing partnerships around a real shared problem last.

### 3. Freedom of religion

What Mawlānā had to fight for as a condition, Britain already gives us by law. Freedom of religion is protected under the Human Rights Act and the Equality Act. The Metropolitan Police has allowed the hijab in uniform since the early 2000s. Sikhs have been exempt from wearing motorcycle helmets since 1976. There are state-funded Muslim schools, Muslim chaplains in hospitals, prisons and the armed forces, and ḥalāl slaughter is permitted.

Compare that with France. In 2004 it banned religious symbols, including the hijab, in state schools. In 2010 it banned face coverings in public. In 2023 it banned the abaya in schools, and at the Paris 2024 Olympics French athletes weren’t allowed to wear the hijab. The point isn’t to criticise France, but to show that Britain has chosen to accommodate faith rather than push it out of public life. Our job is to appreciate that freedom, use it well, and stand up for it for everyone.

### 4. Better than the alternatives

Mawlānā accepted a joint state honestly, not as an Islamic system, but as the best option available. British Muslims are in a similar place. We can’t expect a Muslim state. Muslims are 6.5% of the population here, and we live here by choice and by agreement. Mawlānā himself pointed out that many collective rulings of the sharīʿah are addressed to rulers, not to communities living without Islamic government (Composite Nationalism and Islam, pp. 133–134). But we also can’t step back from politics altogether. He warned that keeping Muslims out of political life “would not only cripple them, but would also lead them to their graves” (p. 151). If we step back, others make the decisions about our families, schools and mosques for us. So what’s left is active, engaged citizenship. It isn’t the ideal, but it’s the option that leaves the most room for faith, service and good.

### 5. Legal protections

In India these safeguards still had to be won. Here they already exist: equality law, human rights law, and charity law protecting mosques and trusts. What we need to do is know these rights, use them properly, and defend them for every faith community, not just ours, because a right that protects one community protects all of them.

### 6. Autonomy at a community level

There’s nothing like state-level autonomy here, and nobody is asking for it. The equivalent today is communities organising their own affairs well within the law: well-run mosques and madrasahs, Muslim charities and schools, burial committees, and ways of resolving family and business matters by agreement, all done openly under British law. A community that runs its own affairs well needs less from the state and has more to give back.

### 7. Education

Mawlānā’s approach still works: protect religious education, and take part in shared education. Britain allows madrasahs, Muslim schools and Darul Ulooms, alongside mainstream schools, colleges and universities. Muslims should aim to excel in both, and stay involved in what’s taught through school governors and positive dialogue with schools.

### 8. Moral and civic effort

Getting involved politically isn’t the end of the work. Good character, honesty in business, being good neighbours, serving the poor, and showing Islam through our conduct are the things that last, and they’re what make a community trusted.

### 9. Foreign and public policy

British Muslims, like everyone else, care about what happens abroad. The right way to act on that is the way Mawlānā himself chose: lawfully and peacefully, through our MPs, consultations, petitions and humanitarian work, and in the language of justice and international law that every citizen can share.

### 10. Cooperation with conditions

Mawlānā’s cooperation came with conditions, and in Britain most of them are already met: freedom of worship, protection of mosques, freedom in religious education, and equal citizenship. That’s exactly why full civic cooperation makes sense here. The one condition that stays is the one he never let go of: we cooperate in civic life, but we never compromise on our faith.
