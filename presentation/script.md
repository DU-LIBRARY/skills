# Mawlānā Madanī’s ten applications, and Britain today

All ten points come from a letter Mawlānā Madanī raḥimahullāh wrote to Ḥāfiẓ Muḥammad Ṣiddīq, which is printed near the end of Naqsh-e-Hayat. I’ve checked each one against the letter itself, and where our summary didn’t quite match what he wrote, I’ve noted it.

## How he applied it to India

### 1. Standing against oppression, but peacefully

Mawlānā starts by saying that British rule over India was oppressive, and that Muslims had a duty to work towards freeing the country. But he’s realistic about it. Muslims, he says, simply don’t have the strength to remove this government by force, so the duty on every Muslim right now is to struggle peacefully: freedom is the goal, and peaceful agitation is how to get there.

(Note for us: in the letter he first mentions that, in principle, resistance can range from boycotts to armed struggle, and only then narrows it to peaceful means. He also calls India “Darul Harb”, but only as a technical fiqh term, and only for as long as British rule lasts. In front of the MP, either explain it that way or leave it out.)

### 2. Strategic unity

Then he asks the obvious question: what happens if Muslims take on the British on their own? They lose, and they suffer badly for it. So they have to join hands with India’s other communities, and because everyone struggles together, everyone shares in the freedom at the end.

### 3. Equal citizenship in a joint state

He’s honest about what this new India would look like. Whether it ends up unitary or federal, it will be a joint government in which Muslims and non-Muslims have equal rights and equal duties. He says plainly that a system like this “cannot be called the Islamic system of government.” For me, that honesty is one of the most striking things in the letter.

### 4. Choosing the lesser evil

Why accept it, then? Because it’s the lesser evil. A free, united India gives Muslims far more room to practise and share their faith than British rule ever did. He also argues it’s a lesser evil than Partition, because dividing the country would let Britain play India and Pakistan off against each other. As for the fear of living alongside a non-Muslim majority, he says it’s unfounded.

### 5. Constitutional and legal protections

He isn’t naive, though. He wants it all written down: a democratic constitution, with an elected President serving a fixed term, sometimes a Muslim and sometimes not, and without the absolute powers of a king. Muslims would have guaranteed political, economic and religious rights. Where they’re a minority they keep their personal law, and the law protects their mosques, awqāf, graveyards and culture.

(Note for us: our original summary said the presidency would rotate, but the letter only says it would sometimes be a Muslim and sometimes not.)

### 6. Local and regional autonomy

Next he turns to where power should sit. The central government should handle only the big things: defence, foreign affairs, transport and some economic matters. Social and cultural affairs go to the provinces, and the five Muslim-majority provinces would be free to make their own laws on education, the economy and social life. A limited centre lets people shape the things closest to their own lives.

### 7. Educational freedom and engagement

Education, he says, should be a provincial matter. Where Muslims are a minority, they can arrange their own religious education, even if that means paying extra or running their own institutions. He also has no problem with Muslims studying alongside everyone else, as long as the syllabus doesn’t clash with Islamic values, because staying away from cooperation with others in the country is, as he puts it, “quite impossible.”

### 8. The work carries on

He’s clear that a joint government won’t bring about Islamic law, and that “the lesser evil cannot be the ultimate goal for Muslims.” Independence isn’t the finish line. Daʿwah, reform and setting a good example all carry on, and in his words, “The road to action is open for Muslims.” He even proposes Iṣlāḥ (reform) centres to look after the community’s values.

### 9. A principled foreign policy

He also looks beyond India’s borders, because for him independence is more than a national issue. If Muslims have a real say in central government, run several provinces and have good ties with neighbouring Muslim countries, India’s foreign policy will be friendly to the Muslim world, and free Indian Muslims will be much better placed to help Muslims elsewhere.

### 10. Conditional cooperation

Finally, he sums it up. Working with non-Muslims for independence is, in his words, “permissible and necessary,” but only on conditions: real Muslim participation in government, protection of personal law, legal protection for mosques, awqāf and graveyards, and Muslim-majority government in five of the eleven provinces. It isn’t a blank cheque.

## What this looks like in Britain today

### 1. Something that unites us

In Mawlānā’s India, people were brought together by a shared burden: colonial rule. Britain has no oppressor to unite against. What unites us instead are the everyday problems that hit Muslims and their neighbours alike. Leaving immigration aside, the latest ONS figures (August 2026) show what people care about most: the cost of living (89%), the NHS (79%), the economy (70%), climate change (62%), crime (58%) and housing (54%). For young people, housing and jobs come up just as often. No community can fix any of these on its own, and every community suffers when they’re left.

### 2. Working with other communities

Some practical ideas, and the problems we’d need to think through:

- Cost of living: food banks, warm spaces, and debt or benefits advice, run jointly by mosques, churches, gurdwaras, temples and synagogues. We shouldn’t duplicate what already exists, so it’s better to partner with local providers, and the work has to last beyond the winter. Help for non-Muslims comes from general charity (ṣadaqah), not zakāh.
- The NHS and health: health days at the mosque with local NHS teams, and encouraging young people into NHS careers. Scholars differ on some health topics, like organ donation, so our role there is to inform and refer people to the ʿulamāʾ, not to campaign. Women’s health work needs to be led by women.
- Crime and youth safety: mentoring, sport and safe spaces for young people across communities, and good relationships with the police and council. Trust in the police isn’t the same in every community, so it has to be built steadily. Everything needs proper safeguarding, and we should check whether it’s actually working.
- Housing and jobs: housing advice sessions, CV clinics, and apprenticeships with local employers.
- Trust in politics: joint hustings and voter registration drives. Mosques and faith charities have to stay strictly non-party-political, though, because charity law doesn’t allow them to back a party or candidate.

A few principles run through all of this. First, we cooperate in civic life, not in worship; that’s Mawlānā’s own line between qawm and millat. Second, it has to involve women, young people and families, not just a handful of community “leaders”. Third, relationships need to be built before a crisis, as Leicester in 2022 showed. And one-off interfaith events fade, while partnerships built around a real shared problem last.

### 3. Freedom of religion

What Mawlānā had to fight for as a condition, Britain already gives us by law. Freedom of religion is protected under the Human Rights Act and the Equality Act. The Metropolitan Police has allowed the hijab in uniform since the early 2000s, and Sikhs have been exempt from wearing motorcycle helmets since 1976. There are state-funded Muslim schools, Muslim chaplains in hospitals, prisons and the armed forces, and ḥalāl slaughter is permitted.

France has gone the other way. In 2004 it banned religious symbols, including the hijab, in state schools. In 2010 it banned face coverings in public. In 2023 it banned the abaya in schools, and at the Paris 2024 Olympics French athletes weren’t allowed to wear the hijab. We’re not raising this to criticise France. It shows that Britain has chosen to make room for faith in public life rather than push it out, and our job is to use that freedom well and defend it for everyone.

### 4. Better than the alternatives

Mawlānā accepted a joint state as the best option available, and was honest that it wasn’t an Islamic system. British Muslims are in a similar position. We can’t expect a Muslim state: Muslims are 6.5% of the population here, and we live here by choice and by agreement. Mawlānā himself pointed out that many collective rulings of the sharīʿah are addressed to rulers, not to communities living without Islamic government (Composite Nationalism and Islam, pp. 133–134).

But we can’t step back from politics altogether either. He warned that keeping Muslims out of political life “would not only cripple them, but would also lead them to their graves” (p. 151). If we step back, others make the decisions about our families, schools and mosques for us. That leaves active, engaged citizenship. Like his joint state, it isn’t the ideal, but it leaves the most room to practise our faith and serve others.

### 5. Legal protections

In India these safeguards still had to be won. Here they already exist: equality law, human rights law, and charity law protecting mosques and trusts. Our part is to know these rights, use them properly, and defend them for every faith community, not only ours, since a right that protects one community protects the others too.

### 6. Autonomy at a community level

There’s nothing like state-level autonomy here, and nobody is asking for it. The nearest equivalent is communities running their own affairs well and within the law: well-run mosques and madrasahs, Muslim charities and schools, burial committees, and ways of settling family and business matters by agreement, all done openly under British law. A community that manages its own affairs well needs less from the state and has more to give back.

### 7. Education

Mawlānā’s approach still works: protect religious education, and take part in shared education. Britain allows madrasahs, Muslim schools and Darul Ulooms alongside mainstream schools, colleges and universities. Muslims should aim to excel in both, and stay involved in what’s taught by serving as school governors and talking constructively with schools.

### 8. Moral and civic effort

As in his eighth point, political involvement isn’t the end of the work. Good character, honesty in business, being good neighbours, serving the poor and showing Islam through our conduct are what last, and they’re what make a community trusted.

### 9. Foreign and public policy

British Muslims, like everyone else, care about what happens abroad. The right way to act on that is the way Mawlānā chose: lawfully and peacefully, through our MPs, consultations, petitions and humanitarian work, and in the language of justice and international law that every citizen can share.

### 10. Cooperation with conditions

Mawlānā’s cooperation came with conditions, and in Britain most of them are already met: freedom of worship, protection of mosques, freedom in religious education, and equal citizenship. That’s why full civic cooperation makes sense here. The one condition that stays is the one he never let go of: we cooperate in civic life, and we never compromise on our faith.
